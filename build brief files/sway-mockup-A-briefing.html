Chapter 01

Who are they

A human description of who they are, what they care about, and what makes them influential.
absorbs: Influential Core tab · Snapshot core summary

Use this chapter to brief anyone — creative, client, or planner — on who this audience actually is.

5–10% of ~12M
The influential core

The Friend Who Crafts

The one you text before buying yarn — and who sometimes tells you not to.
Earns authority by — candor: confessed stashes, unfinished projects, refusal to monetize
Trusted because — zero commercial stake; recommendations specific enough to verify
Hard to reach with — ads, influencers, polish; sponsorship is detected instantly
Easy to reach through — yarn shops, knit nights, Ravelry, small candid newsletters
How we sized the core
Ravelry active-contributor layer vs. registered accounts; LYS regulars vs. occasional walk-ins per retail coverage; pattern-designer follower distributions. Triangulated, not measured. derivation · 4 sources · verified
The base — how they behave
  • Enter through kits, trends and viral video; drift between crafts seasonally
  • Shop on price and access — big-box first, specialty later if ever
  • Follow recommendations — the core's picks arrive secondhand, stripped of source
How the core differs
  • Never mentions kits; rejects craft-as-identity and hustle culture outright
  • Buys from independent dyers and LYS — provenance over price
  • Produces the recommendations the base copies; initiates, never follows

The Rhinebeck sweater ritual

A garment genre named for one festival — knit all year, debuted in one field in October, legible only to insiders.
The fiber festival circuit is where the core's status economy becomes visible: a handmade sweater worn at Rhinebeck is a résumé, a conversation opener, and an invitation to be photographed for the community's own channels. Influence here moves through recognition, not reach.

Don't-buy content as the highest-trust format

Only in fiber craft does a yarn seller telling customers not to buy yarn — including her own — build the deepest credibility.
Restraint content (deinfluencing, no-buy years, stash-first pledges) out-performs enthusiasm across every channel the core inhabits. For brands the implication is inverted marketing: credibility is earned by helping people buy less, better.

Chapter 04

How to move them

The emotional drivers, motivations, tensions and triggers most likely to change behavior.
absorbs: Motivational tab (drivers · tensions · triggers)

Use this chapter to brief creative and messaging — what to lean on, and what will get you rejected.

Primary driver

Permission

Release from productivity pressure — the craft that doesn't have to become anything.
The strongest motivational lever in the research. This audience is surrounded by messaging that says every hobby should become content, income, or self-improvement; the brands that win here explicitly grant the opposite. "It doesn't have to be good, it just has to be yours" is the emotional register.
Confidence Converged — all three lenses
Show evidence
Anti-hustle sentiment across community discussions; "process knitter vs. product knitter" self-identification; rejection patterns around monetization prompts. 3 lenses · verified

Belongingcraft is the excuse; the circle is the productStrong
Knit nights, library groups, festival meetups — the social container motivates continuation more than the output does. Sell the gathering, not the object.
Shared accountabilitystash guilt resolved communally — never through more buyingStrong
No-buy pledges and stash-down challenges are performed publicly. The tension between acquisition and restraint is the audience's central emotional drama — and the trigger moment brands can respectfully join.
Tactile decompressioncraft as the antidote to screens and deadlinesModerate
Repetitive handwork as regulation; the "knitting in meetings helps me focus" discourse. Positions craft against digital exhaustion, not against other hobbies.
Human provenancemade-by-hand as identity — sharpened by anti-AI sentimentEmerging
The value of the handmade is rising as generated content floods every feed; "a human made this" is becoming this audience's premium claim.
Chapter 05

How to engage them

The content, experiences, partnerships and participation mechanics most likely to get them to act.
absorbs: Activation section · entry points — currently our thinnest layer; depth is a Maria-checkpoint question

Use this chapter to choose formats and mechanics — the ways in that this audience actually accepts.

Content & formats
  • Candor formats — unfinished work, failures, real stashes; polish is disqualifying
  • Stash-first campaigns — help them use what they own; earn the next purchase
  • Process over product — the making is the story, not the finished object
Partnerships & participation
  • LYS partnerships — exclusive colorways, knit-night sponsorship, LYS Day presence
  • Pattern collabs with trusted independent designers — pay the core, don't rent it
  • Festival presence — be photographed, not advertised; sponsor the ritual, never interrupt it
